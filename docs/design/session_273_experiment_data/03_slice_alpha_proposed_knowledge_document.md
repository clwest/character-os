## Breads & sourdough

Sourdough is the foundation of Fox & Flour. Ruby maintains two active
starters that live in the walk-in fridge and get fed the night before
each bake day. The house loaf is a naturally leavened country
sourdough with a mixed-flour crumb — mostly bread flour with roughly
15% whole wheat — proofed overnight and baked in a deck oven for a
dark, blistered crust. The country sourdough is available every day
Fox & Flour is open.

Rotating alongside the country loaf are two or three other breads
depending on the week: a rustic olive-and-rosemary loaf that
customers ask about by name, a rye-caraway sandwich loaf that most
weeks sells out by mid-afternoon, and in cooler months a
dark-chocolate-and-sour-cherry sourdough that goes out on Fridays
only. A soft brioche is baked twice weekly for the pastry program.

Whole loaves are priced by weight and cut on request. Half loaves are
available. Day-old bread is discounted the following morning; when
it doesn't sell it goes to the local food shelf on Sunday evening.

## Pastries

The morning pastry case is baked fresh each morning starting at 3 AM
and put out by 7 AM. The daily lineup usually includes:

- All-butter croissants (plain and almond)
- Kouign-amann in three flavors that rotate seasonally
- Cardamom-sugar buns
- A galette or hand pie built on whatever fruit is best that week
- Two or three cookies (chocolate chunk is always one of them; the
  other spots rotate)
- Scones on the weekend only

Pastries are not held over. What doesn't sell by close is either
marked half-price in the last hour or donated the same evening.

## Celebration cakes

Custom celebration cakes are Fox & Flour's flagship special-order
work. Ruby designs the cakes herself and executes most of them with
one part-time decorator on the weekends. The order lead time is a
minimum of five days for standard tiered cakes and ten days for
sculpted or heavily decorated cakes; peak seasons (spring weddings,
December holidays) push those windows out further.

Available base cake styles include vanilla-bean chiffon, brown-butter
almond, chocolate devil's food, olive oil citrus, and hummingbird.
Fillings range from Swiss meringue buttercream in a dozen or so
flavors to fresh curds, whipped ganache, and seasonal fruit compotes.
Fondant is available but Ruby steers most customers toward buttercream
finishes; the shop's aesthetic leans toward rustic-elegant rather
than sharp-fondant.

A 50% deposit is taken at booking. Deposits are non-refundable inside
of 72 hours before pickup. Fox & Flour does not deliver cakes; all
orders are pickup only.

## Ingredients & sourcing

Flour comes from a Colorado-based regional mill. Butter and dairy are
sourced from a family creamery two hours north. Eggs are from a
pastured farm the same distance. Fruit shifts with the season —
during summer and fall most fruit comes through the Wednesday and
Saturday farmers markets from three growers Ruby has worked with for
years; in winter Ruby supplements with citrus from a California
cooperative. Chocolate is single-origin from a small West Coast
chocolate maker. Salt is flaky sea salt from Maine.

Ruby is intentional about not marketing "artisan" as a buzzword. She
prefers to name the actual farm or mill when a customer asks. The
bakery's habit is: if it's on the menu, Ruby can tell you where it
came from.

## Bakery routines

Fox & Flour is open Wednesday through Sunday, 7 AM to 3 PM. Monday
and Tuesday are closed to the public and used for large custom-order
production, deep cleaning, and menu development.

The baking rhythm is: mixed doughs the afternoon before, bench proof
overnight, shape and bake early morning. Pastry lamination happens on
Sundays for the following week. Custom-cake baking, filling, and
decoration happens Monday, Tuesday, and Thursday.

Ruby does the morning bake herself on Wednesday, Friday, and Sunday.
Her lead baker covers Thursday and Saturday. There are two counter
staff and one part-time decorator.

## Seasonal offerings

Menu rotations follow the calendar loosely:

- **Spring:** rhubarb galettes, strawberry-basil cake, hot-cross buns
  the week before Easter, an asparagus-and-goat-cheese savory scone.
- **Summer:** stone-fruit hand pies, cherry-almond kouign-amann,
  seasonal fruit shortcakes for weekend pickup.
- **Autumn:** apple-brown-butter tarts, pumpkin-cardamom loaves,
  pear-and-hazelnut sourdough, sweet-potato dinner rolls for
  Thanksgiving preorders.
- **Winter:** citrus everything (bergamot, blood orange, meyer lemon),
  gingerbread and cardamom bakes through December, king cakes in
  January.

Preorder holidays (Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, Easter) have a
published cutoff two weeks ahead and no walk-in same-day availability
for the specialty items.

## Customer experience

The bakery is a small storefront with six seats at the counter and a
handful of two-tops by the window. Ruby knows most regulars by name
and by their usual order — the shop keeps a private paper notebook
behind the register for allergy notes and children's-birthday
histories. Newcomers who look overwhelmed by the case are gently
walked through it; Ruby's rule for her staff is that no question
about the menu is too basic to answer patiently.

Fox & Flour maintains a small waitlist for celebration cakes during
peak weeks. When the shop can't take a booking, the counter staff
recommend one of two other independent bakers in town who Ruby knows
and trusts.

Coffee is served but not roasted in-house — Fox & Flour buys beans
from a small roaster nearby. The pairing between coffee and pastry is
part of the case-walkthrough conversation for newer customers.

## Ruby's reason for operating Fox & Flour

Ruby opened Fox & Flour after eight years of working in other
people's kitchens — first in a fine-dining pastry program in a bigger
city, then in a bakery-café that got sold to a chain. The chain
experience is what pushed her to open her own place: she wanted the
bakery to belong to the neighborhood rather than to a distant owner
who never smelled the ovens. The name comes from an old family
nickname (Ruby's grandmother called her "little fox") and from the
literal thing the shop is about (flour).

The bakery is deliberately small. Ruby has been offered the chance to
expand or open a second location and has said no both times. Her view
is that the ceiling on quality moves down when she stops being in the
kitchen every morning, and she is not willing to move that ceiling
just to grow.

She wants customers to leave with something they'll remember eating.
That's the whole point.
