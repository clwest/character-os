# Visual Territory Decision Bridge

## v1 — canonical selection of visual foundation

*Anchored in [`VISUAL_LANGUAGE_V1_2026-08-06.md`](../VISUAL_LANGUAGE_V1_2026-08-06.md) and [`HOME_VISUAL_V1_2026-08-06.md`](HOME_VISUAL_V1_2026-08-06.md). Three genuinely distinct visual territories were drafted, compared, and one was selected as the foundation for Character OS's visual language. Editorial and Gallery are preserved as scoped disciplines within the Workshop foundation.*

This document does not lock fonts, colors, tokens, spacing, components, or Figma layouts. It locks the **hierarchy of visual disciplines** that will govern every subsequent study.

---

## Locked decision

**Workshop is the foundation. Editorial is the discipline. Gallery is the moment.**

- **Workshop** defines how the product feels day to day — warmth, craftsmanship, tangible work, honest materials, calm confidence, the register of a well-designed studio where making happens.
- **Editorial** defines how language, hierarchy, and reading quality are executed within that room — typographic rigor, hierarchy discipline, reading comfort as a first-class concern.
- **Gallery** defines how finished work is honored during moments of accomplishment — generous space, reverent presentation, the piece as work-on-a-wall when the customer stops to look.

Every future visual decision — every color, every type choice, every layout, every animation, every avatar treatment — is evaluated against this three-part hierarchy in this order. Workshop wins on daily register; Editorial wins on language and reading; Gallery wins on pride moments.

---

## Why this hierarchy

Character OS is fundamentally about **making** — not publishing, not showcasing, not reading. Every screen eventually points back toward making another thing. If the core metaphor is not compatible with making, the visual language fights itself. Workshop is the only territory of the three that supports making natively.

But Workshop alone is not enough. Premium products are recognized more by typography and reading quality than by buttons or colors. The examples that carry the reference — Apple, Notion, Linear, Readwise, Arc — are recognizable through language and space, not chrome. Editorial discipline inside a Workshop foundation is the combination the reference class has proven works.

Gallery serves the rarer but critical moments where the customer stops to admire what they made. Philosophy #6 (*the customer's work is displayed as pride, not inventory*) requires those moments to feel genuinely reverential. Gallery's spatial generosity is the visual mechanism that makes those moments land.

---

## What this hierarchy specifically means

- **On Home ordinary-return, Studio, My Team, and Live Conversation:** Workshop leads. The register is a warm, capable working environment where the teammate feels nearby and the customer's next action is unmistakably clear.
- **On typography, reading surfaces, transcripts, knowledge prose, and all instances where language carries meaning:** Editorial discipline governs. Line length, paragraph rhythm, hierarchy, quote treatment, metadata voice, and the general reading feel of the product are treated with the care a well-run publication would apply.
- **On Home celebration, Piece Detail, published-work moments in Library, and My Team teammate portrait treatment:** Gallery moments appear. Space opens up. The featured element receives room to be seen.

A specific screen may express one, two, or all three disciplines simultaneously, but the *dominant* register at any moment follows the hierarchy: workshop unless a language-heavy moment (editorial rises) or a pride moment (gallery rises) genuinely warrants otherwise.

---

## The literal-metaphor caution

The Workshop metaphor lives at the **emotional** level, not the decorative level.

Forbidden in service of workshop:

- **Wood-grain textures**, real or simulated, as background or panel treatment.
- **Leather** textures.
- **"Tool rack" UI patterns** — literal-looking racks, hooks, hanging affordances.
- **Craft-store visual clichés** — screws, paper clips, tape, clipboards, twine, kraft paper edges, tape-corner effects, sticky notes as UI.
- **Workshop still-life imagery** — coffee mugs on the surface, pencils in a jar, hand-drawn illustrations that imply the workshop is *displayed* rather than *implied*.

The metaphor exists as: warmth, craftsmanship, tangible work, honest materials, calm confidence. If someone can identify the metaphor by spotting visual props, it has gone too far. The workshop must feel like a room where work happens, not a themed set decorated to look like one.

---

## What must not be combined

- **Gallery's coolness with Workshop's warmth** — creates identity confusion. Pick one temperature.
- **Editorial's preciousness with Workshop's material feel** — reads as *"trying too hard to look designed."* Editorial discipline should be invisible (well-set type, clear hierarchy), not visible (mannered layouts, magazine-style flourishes).
- **Any two territories' primary signatures** — signatures are singular by definition. Workshop's material warmth is the identity. Editorial's typographic-as-identity signature and Gallery's space-as-identity signature must not compete for that role. They live as supporting character.

# Territory drafts

The three territories below were drafted, compared, and used to arrive at the locked hierarchy. They are preserved here as the reasoning trail. The recommended foundation is Workshop, with Editorial and Gallery scoped as disciplines within it.

---

## Territory 1 — Quiet Editorial Studio

### Core idea

The register of a serious independent magazine or a literary studio — the kind of publication where everything reads like it was edited before it appeared. Typography carries the identity: a well-set page rather than a designed interface. The customer's work is treated as an editorial artifact — like a feature photo essay or a magazine story. The room feels thoughtful, precise, unhurried. This fits Character OS because the product treats the customer's marketing work as *something worth being read carefully*, not as content to be pushed through a pipeline.

### Emotional emphasis

Emphasizes most strongly: **Capable** and **Proud.**
Most at risk: **Curious.** Editorial precision can slip into feeling finished and closed — a magazine, once printed, doesn't invite exploration the way a workshop does. The territory must work to keep discovery alive.

### Typographic character

Strong editorial character. A paired direction is likely — a well-drawn transitional or old-style serif for headings and long-form body reading; a thoughtful sans for interface roles (buttons, labels, small metadata). Alternatively, a single contemporary serif of real quality carrying most of the load, with a matched sans reserved for interface-only work.

- **Heading voice:** composed, quiet authority. Feels like a magazine's opening line.
- **Body voice:** literary — comfortable at length, warm, unhurried.
- **Action-label voice:** precise. Small caps or lightly tracked lowercase; considered.
- **Metadata voice:** bylined, italic, quiet — like a photo credit.

Risks: too precious (editorial slipping into art-house); too corporate-magazine; too finished (loss of discovery).

### Tonal environment

Bright but not stark. Paper-white or a very cool off-white (like fine printing paper), not cream. Contrast is selectively high — headings sit strongly against the surface, but the overall register is quiet. Material feel: fine paper.

Primarily light. Glare handled through paper-tone. Cool-lit portraits sit naturally; warm skin tones may need careful surround handling. Customer media compatibility is good but variable — heavily saturated media may feel slightly out of place against the reserved surround.

### Alex treatment

Editorial portrait — bust-length or head-and-shoulders, composed like a magazine contributor photograph. Moderate weight. Portrait sits on a subtle tonal panel or feathers into the paper surround. Naturalistic but composed. Minimal subtle life — occasional blinks, gentle breathing.

### Customer-work treatment

Layout-driven, magazine-spread style. The piece has its own well-composed sub-page. Depth mostly through typographic hierarchy and margin. Native aspect ratio preserved. Metadata as bylined italic — like a photo credit. Dignity through editorial composition and typographic care. Rough work reads as an editor's proof.

### Action treatment

Crisp rectangular; considered edges. Solid fill in a restrained accent or considered outline. Contrast unmistakable against paper-white, not saturated. Primary is filled; secondaries outlined or set as text-with-underline in the editorial voice. *Ask for something else* as italic editorial link.

**Rejects:** gradient fills, saturated brand colors, glow, uppercase caps, pill shapes.

### Navigation presence

Small-cap or lightly-tracked labels. Reads more typographically than as chrome. Active state via increased contrast; optionally a small typographic ornament. First-reveal via contrast alone; no motion beyond appearance. Like the top-of-page index in a well-designed magazine.

**Not SaaS:** no icons, no active-state pill fills, no accent-strip indicators.

### Spatial rhythm

Comfortable-editorial density — more air than a dashboard, less than a gallery. Margins generous but proportioned to reading. Vertical pacing like a page layout. Greeting and Alex form a masthead; actions form a considered call-out; the piece is the feature. Celebration adds warmer accent and heightened typographic hierarchy.

### Motion character

Restrained. Editorial. Almost typographic. Elements settle like ink drying. Forbidden: springs, elastic, dramatic entrances. Reduced motion: page composes itself instantly; editorial rhythm reads without animation.

### Distinguishing signature

**The typography.** Someone would identify this territory instantly by how the text is set — character of type, care of composition, confidence of hierarchy. Type is the identity.

### Primary risks

1. **Too magazine-like to feel interactive** — customers may not register that things are tappable.
2. **Too precious** — editorial register can slip into "trying too hard."
3. **Portrait environment friction** — some generated portraits may not sit gracefully in the cool-paper environment.

### Best-fit screens

**Naturally benefits:** Home (celebration), Library, Piece.
**Greatest adaptation required:** Live Conversation. Editorial environments are quiet; live avatar conversations need warmer, denser, more embodied presence.

### Home snapshot (ordinary return)

Alex sits at the top like a contributor portrait — bust-length, quiet expression. Beside Alex, a masthead-styled greeting: *"Welcome back. You left off in the middle of your welcome video — want to finish it?"* set in a warm serif with real character. Below, **[Continue your welcome video]** as a considered rectangular button, edges crisp, fill restrained. Secondary options as quieter text-and-underline treatments. The recent published video in its own well-composed sub-page. Italic byline: *"Published Tuesday · Alex."* Navigation reads like a magazine's spine.

## Territory 2 — Warm Contemporary Workshop

### Core idea

The register of a physical workshop that's been thoughtfully designed — a good ceramicist's studio, a small woodshop, an artisanal print studio. Materials speak. Warmth is tactile rather than decorative. The piece is treated as visible craft — something made by hand, resting on a well-lit table. The customer feels they're at their own workbench, with tools that fit their hand. This fits Character OS because the product is fundamentally about *making things* — the workshop register is the honest metaphor for what the customer actually does.

### Emotional emphasis

Emphasizes most strongly: **Capable** and **Supported.**
Most at risk: **Proud.** Workshop registers can feel productive rather than reverential. The territory must invest in moments where finished pieces receive room to be looked at.

### Typographic character

Classification-neutral or humanist-sans direction — with more warmth and slightly softer forms than an editorial serif. A single well-drawn contemporary sans could carry most of the load; a matched slab or grotesque might accent. Alternatively, a pairing of a warm sans with a small-set serif for special typographic moments.

- **Heading voice:** warm and grounded — like a friendly headline in a craft or design magazine.
- **Body voice:** comfortable, present, direct. Less literary than editorial.
- **Action-label voice:** direct, sentence-case, moderate weight.
- **Metadata voice:** quiet, casual, natural — a maker's note.

Risks: too casual; too "startup"; too generic-humanist.

### Tonal environment

Warm off-white or a subtle warm-tinted background — like well-lit natural surfaces. Contrast comfortable rather than sharp. Material feel: warm wood, unbleached paper, ceramic — texture implied without being decorated.

Primarily light, with strong possibility of adaptation to warm-dark surfaces for specific screens. Glare handled naturally through warm-neutral surfaces. Portrait compatibility excellent for warm skin tones; very good for warm-lit media; may need care with cool-lit portraits.

### Alex treatment

Closer than editorial — head-and-shoulders or slightly wider, like sitting across from someone at a workbench. Present but not monumental. Portrait sits on the surface with soft ambient shadow. Naturalistic with warmth. Fuller subtle life — blinks, breathing, small posture shifts.

### Customer-work treatment

The piece rests "on the workbench" — often on a subtle tonal panel that reads as a work surface. Soft ambient shadows and tonal shifts imply the piece rests on something material. Native aspect ratio preserved. Metadata as quiet handwritten note. Dignity through material framing and warm ambient light. Rough work looks like clay on a wheel — unfinished but present.

### Action treatment

Soft rectangular, slightly rounded corners — approachable but not pill-shaped. Solid fill in a grounded accent — perhaps a deep muted color that reads as material rather than brand. Contrast unmistakable but warm. Outlined siblings with the same rounded profile.

**Rejects:** cold blue links, saturated primary colors, hard-cornered aggressive shapes, uppercase caps, glow, glass surfaces.

### Navigation presence

Small warm strip with labels only — quiet, tangible, part of the room. Subtle warm fill or increased contrast for active state. First-reveal with slightly stronger initial presence, one gentle motion cue, then settles.

**Not SaaS:** no dashboard rail with icons; no vertical accent bar; no active-state pill fills; no dark chrome.

### Spatial rhythm

Comfortable density — more furnished than a gallery, less than a workshop floor. Margins generous where the customer is looking; tighter where controls need reachability. Alex-and-greeting feel like a colleague sitting nearby; actions feel like tools at hand; the piece is the work. Celebration adds a moment of held warm light.

### Motion character

Tactile. Grounded. Elements settle like an object being placed on a table. Soft appearance with slight scale-in coming to rest. Forbidden: springy overshoot, elastic bounces, decorative flair, glow, shimmer. Reduced motion: static composition still reads as tactile through material treatment.

### Distinguishing signature

**Material warmth.** The collective sense that the room has material presence. Not through any single element — through the room's ambient feel.

### Primary risks

1. **Too warm/handmade to feel technically capable** — customers may not trust it with sophisticated marketing work.
2. **Too casual** — workshop register can slip into "friendly startup."
3. **Consistency across a growing product surface** — material warmth is easier on a few crafted screens than across a full system.

### Best-fit screens

**Naturally benefits:** Home (both tempos), Studio, My Team.
**Greatest adaptation required:** Library at scale — a workbench gets crowded; the metaphor may need reinterpretation for 100+ pieces.

### Home snapshot (ordinary return)

The room feels like the customer walked back to their own workbench. Alex sits nearby — closer than a portrait, like a colleague at the next stool — warm quiet expression, breathing gently. Greeting reads as spoken: *"Welcome back. You left off in the middle of your welcome video — want to finish it?"* Below, **[Continue your welcome video]** as a rounded, grounded button, solid fill in a deep material color, edges soft. Secondary actions in the same rounded family, quieter. The recent published video on a subtle tonal panel that reads as a work surface. Small quiet note: *"Published Tuesday · Alex."* Navigation as a small warm strip.

## Territory 3 — Refined Human Gallery

### Core idea

The register of a small, thoughtfully curated gallery — the kind of contemporary art space where each work gets its own wall and the room breathes around it. Generosity of space is the identity. The piece is treated as work on a wall, deserving attention and distance. The customer feels they've entered a considered space where their work matters and there is room to see it. This fits Character OS because the pride principle is best served by a room that treats every published piece as if it *belongs* there.

### Emotional emphasis

Emphasizes most strongly: **Proud** and **Calm.**
Most at risk: **Capable.** Gallery registers may feel too finished, less like a working environment.

### Typographic character

Classification-neutral with a bias toward highly-crafted contemporary sans workhorses. A paired grotesque (for interface and body) with an elegant serif reserved for hero-only moments. Alternatively, a single well-crafted contemporary sans doing most work.

- **Heading voice:** quietly elegant. Small in size relative to the space around it, composed with real care.
- **Body voice:** highly readable, generous line-length, direct. Not literary.
- **Action-label voice:** precise, moderate weight. Spare.
- **Metadata voice:** very quiet, small — like museum wall labels.

Risks: too neutral (loss of distinct character); too "startup contemporary"; too austere.

### Tonal environment

Bright but softened — like light through a north-facing gallery window. Not stark, not warm. Contrast selective. Material feel: soft wall paint rather than paper or wood.

Adaptive possibility strongest here — the gallery could exist in light-mode and dark-mode surrounds. Portrait and customer-media compatibility is the strongest of the three territories.

### Alex treatment

Composed portrait, bust-length; treated as a person represented on a wall. Substantial but framed by generous space. Portrait sits on the ambient wall with subtle depth. Naturalistic and considered — like a portrait commissioned for a specific space. Minimal subtle life.

### Customer-work treatment

Containment implied by generous space around the work, not by a container. The piece has its own "wall." Minimal depth. Native aspect ratio preserved. Metadata as museum wall labels. Dignity through space and light. Rough work presented plainly.

### Action treatment

Precise rectangular with fine edges. Solid fill in a considered accent. Unmistakable through weight, placement, and contrast rather than through saturation. Outlined siblings.

**Rejects:** pill shapes, gradient fills, corner radius taken too far, uppercase all-caps, glow, shimmer.

### Navigation presence

Very quiet — labels only, small, generously spaced. Subtle contrast increase or fine indicator (small dot, underline). First-reveal via contrast alone, no motion.

**Not SaaS:** no icons, no bold active visuals, no vertical strip fills, no chrome-heavy indicators.

### Spatial rhythm

The most spatially-generous of the three territories. Margins vast, especially around the featured piece. Unhurried vertical pacing. Each element has its own visual "wall" separated by generous space. Celebration adds a moment where the piece receives slightly warmer ambient light.

### Motion character

Architectural. Elements settle like a curtain being drawn in a well-lit room. Barely perceptible. Reduced motion: composition works entirely without motion — space is doing the work.

### Distinguishing signature

**Space.** Someone would identify this territory by the *proportions* — the sense that everything has room.

### Primary risks

1. **Too gallery-like to support daily work** — a returning customer with 8 things to do may find generous space fatiguing.
2. **Too neutral to feel ownable** — gallery register can feel like *any* refined product.
3. **Space cost on smaller viewports** — mobile risks cramping or scroll-heaviness.

### Best-fit screens

**Naturally benefits:** Home (celebration), Piece, My Team.
**Greatest adaptation required:** Studio — needs denser controls and productive-feeling working surface. Gallery register may resist Studio's intensity.

### Home snapshot (ordinary return)

The room breathes. Alex sits high in the visual field — considered, quiet, framed by generous space. Greeting: *"Welcome back. You left off in the middle of your welcome video — want to finish it?"* in a clean contemporary voice, sized modestly against the space. Below, **[Continue your welcome video]** as a precise rectangular button, solid fill in a considered accent, edges fine. The recent published video occupies its own wall, surrounded by generous margin. Museum-label style note: *"Published Tuesday · Alex."* Navigation reads as almost invisible.

# Comparison

Relative judgments, not scores. `>` means *stronger in.*

| Dimension | Ranking |
|---|---|
| **Calm** | Gallery > Editorial > Workshop |
| **Capability** | Workshop > Editorial > Gallery |
| **Pride** | Gallery ≈ Editorial > Workshop |
| **Curiosity** | Workshop > Gallery > Editorial |
| **Support** | Workshop > Editorial ≈ Gallery |
| **Editorial character** | Editorial > Gallery > Workshop |
| **Portrait compatibility** | Gallery > Workshop > Editorial |
| **Customer-media compatibility** | Gallery > Workshop > Editorial |
| **Daily usability** | Workshop > Editorial > Gallery |
| **Risk of SaaS resemblance** | Gallery highest; Editorial moderate; Workshop lowest |
| **Risk of becoming overly decorative** | Editorial highest; Workshop moderate; Gallery lowest |
| **Ability to scale across all seven screens** | Workshop > Gallery > Editorial |

Reading the matrix:

- **Editorial** wins on editorial character and pride, but is the hardest to scale (Live Conversation and Studio require substantial adaptation) and carries the greatest preciousness risk.
- **Workshop** wins on daily usability, support, curiosity, and scaling. It has the lowest SaaS risk and the highest fit for the screens customers spend most time in.
- **Gallery** wins on pride, calm, portrait compatibility, and customer-media compatibility. It is the most beautiful for celebration and Piece, but the least productive for daily use.

The matrix does not point at a single winner across every dimension. It points at a hierarchy: **use Workshop as the foundation, use Editorial as the discipline that governs language and reading, use Gallery as the moment that appears when finished work is being honored.** All three contribute; Workshop leads.

# Recommendation

Locked at the top of this document. Restated here for symmetry:

**Workshop is the foundation. Editorial is the discipline. Gallery is the moment.**

## Why this hierarchy carries all seven screens

- **Character OS is a daily tool.** The retention arc — the second of the two customer journey arcs — is where the customer spends most of their time. The celebration moment matters, but it happens once per publish. Workshop's productive warmth is the register that best supports the state customers occupy most often.
- **Character OS is a making tool.** Studio is the second-most-visited surface after Home. Only Workshop supports Studio's active-making density natively; Editorial and Gallery both require substantial reinterpretation for that screen.
- **Character OS is a teammate-first product.** Philosophy #2 requires the teammate to feel present, warm, and interruptible. Workshop's "colleague at the next stool" framing serves this better than Editorial's contributor portrait or Gallery's wall portrait. My Team and Live Conversation inherit this framing naturally.
- **Character OS serves small-business customers.** A bakery owner, an HVAC company, a solo bookkeeper understand craft. They may not naturally understand editorial or gallery registers. The workshop metaphor is the most legible across the audience the product is actually for.
- **Philosophy #6** (*the customer's work is displayed as pride, not inventory*) is served in Workshop through *"look what I made"* — closer to how customers actually feel about their own marketing work than *"look what was published"* (Editorial) or *"look what's on display"* (Gallery). Pride, for a maker, comes from the act of making.
- **Editorial discipline covers reading comfort.** Premium products distinguish themselves through typographic care and language treatment more than through chrome. Editorial discipline inside Workshop provides that distinction without importing Editorial's preciousness or scaling problems.
- **Gallery moments cover celebration.** When the customer stops to admire — first publish, viewing a specific piece, browsing their own body of work — the room opens up. Gallery serves those moments precisely, without governing daily usage.

## What must not be combined

- **Gallery's coolness with Workshop's warmth** — creates identity confusion.
- **Editorial's preciousness with Workshop's material feel** — reads as *"trying too hard."* Editorial discipline should be invisible.
- **Any two territories' primary signatures competing for identity** — Workshop's material warmth is the identity. Editorial and Gallery are supporting character.

## What this recommendation does not do

- It does not name a specific typeface, color, or spacing token.
- It does not commit to light-only, dark-only, or adaptive tonal environments (that remains a color-and-light study decision).
- It does not lock any Figma-mappable atom.
- It does not preclude specific screens drifting toward Editorial or Gallery accents where their design work warrants it — Studio remains a workshop; Live Conversation may borrow gallery quiet; Piece may borrow editorial framing. The DNA is Workshop; the local expression is what each screen's design pass decides.

# Next artifacts

Before applying visual language to any specific screen, four studies produce the vocabulary the screens will use:

1. **`PORTRAIT_LANGUAGE_V1_2026-08-06.md`** — how teammates exist visually. Alex is now the product; the portrait system is a first-class design discipline. Covers framing, eye contact, camera distance, shoulder visibility, lighting consistency, expression vocabulary, idle behavior, realism limits, age variation, clothing philosophy, background philosophy, portrait normalization across screens, and how different teammates remain distinct while feeling like they belong to the same product.

2. **`TYPOGRAPHY_STUDY_V1_2026-08-06.md`** — reading comfort and editorial hierarchy. Not typography as decoration; typography as reading discipline. Covers maximum line length, paragraph rhythm, quote styling, transcript styling (Live Conversation, Piece live-conversation records), whether conversation is rendered with bubbles or intentionally without, emphasis hierarchy, inline actions inside prose, metadata voice, and how language behaves everywhere it appears.

3. **`COLOR_AND_LIGHT_V1_2026-08-06.md`** — tonal environment. Tests warm light, warm dark, and adaptive tonal systems against the Workshop foundation. Establishes the principal color's emotional behavior (grounded, inviting, confident, restrained), the surface material, portrait and media compatibility, and light/dark policy.

4. **`HOME_VISUAL_CANONICAL_V1_2026-08-06.md`** — Home's fully-specified visual direction, produced by applying the three studies to Home's V1 visual proposal. This consolidates Home before Onboarding's visual work begins.

This order matters. Producing typography, color, or Home canonical before Portrait Language risks visual decisions that later have to be undone once Alex's identity is fully defined. The portraits are the most fragile visual promise in the product; they must be defined first.

Onboarding's visual proposal becomes the first *screen-specific* visual work after these four studies land.
